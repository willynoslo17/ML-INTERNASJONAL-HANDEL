Incoterms for perishables: CIP and DAP versus FOB or CIF

Introduction

Perishable goods, such as fresh salmon or fruit, raise specific questions about the point of delivery and the transfer of risk. The Incoterms 2020 clearly distinguish between terms valid for any mode of transport and terms exclusive to maritime transport or navigable waterways. Using FOB, CFR or CIF on an air or road shipment is incorrect.

Sector guides, such as that of Brødr. Remø aimed at fish importers, underline this frequent error and discuss the use of CIP or DAP for fresh salmon to Europe. The Incoterm does not “follow the temperature”; it only fixes where and when the risk passes from the seller to the buyer.

Maritime terms misused in air and road

FOB, CFR and CIF require that delivery takes place on board the vessel or, in the case of FAS, alongside the vessel. These concepts make no sense on an aircraft or on a truck. When they are used by error, the parties may disagree on the exact moment when the risk is transferred and on who is responsible for damage occurring during the main transport.

In practice, many perishables contracts are still negotiated with maritime terms by custom, even if the actual transport is air or road. That practice generates legal and operational uncertainty.

CIP and DAP in the context of perishables

CIP (Carriage and Insurance Paid To) and DAP (Delivered at Place) are terms valid for any mode. In CIP the seller delivers to the first carrier and pays the transport and a higher-level insurance (Institute Cargo Clauses A) to the named destination. The risk, however, passes to the buyer at the moment of delivery to the first carrier.

In DAP the seller delivers the goods at the named place of destination, ready for unloading, and assumes the risk until that point. The buyer handles the unloading and the import formalities.

Neither of the two terms is “the correct one” in the abstract. The choice depends on the logistical capacity of each party, on who better controls the transport and on the commercial agreement. No concrete term is recommended here.

Transfer of risk and cold chain

The Incoterm determines the contractual point at which the risk of loss or damage passes to the buyer. If the goods are damaged by a break in the cold chain after that point, the contractual risk corresponds to the buyer, unless the contract has established additional care obligations or there is liability of the carrier.

The obligation to maintain a determined temperature is normally regulated in the sales contract, in the instructions to the carrier and in the transport documents. The Incoterm does not by itself create that obligation nor “follows” it along the chain. Therefore, the parties must coordinate the chosen Incoterm with the quality, temperature and claims clauses.

Can FOB or CIF be used on an air shipment of fresh salmon?
It is not correct. FOB, CFR and CIF are only for maritime transport or navigable waterways. In air and road terms for any mode are used.
Does the Incoterm guarantee that the goods arrive at the correct temperature?
No. The Incoterm fixes the point of delivery and the transfer of risk. The temperature is regulated in the contract and in the instructions to the carrier.
What terms are commonly mentioned for fresh salmon to Europe?
Sector guides discuss CIP or DAP. The concrete choice depends on the agreement between the parties and no single term is recommended here.
Where does the risk pass in CIP?
When the goods are delivered to the first carrier, even if the seller pays the transport to the destination.
